# Why the expectations do not look like mathematics you know

If you read the three curriculum files and did not recognize much, the most likely explanation is not that you have forgotten the mathematics. It is that curriculum expectations are not written in the language of mathematics. They are written in the language of education policy, and they describe classroom activities rather than topics.

Here is an actual expectation, MCR3U A1.8:

> determine, through investigation using technology, the roles of the parameters a, k, d, and c in functions of the form y = af(k(x − d)) + c, and describe these roles in terms of transformations on the graphs of f(x) = x, f(x) = x², f(x) = √x, and f(x) = 1/x

That is forty-five words for "transformations of graphs: stretches, reflections and shifts". You have known that for twenty-five years. You did not recognize it because nobody ever described it to you that way.

This happens on nearly every line. The effect is that a person who knows the material perfectly well reads the document and concludes they know none of it.

## How to read an expectation

**Read the sample problem, not the expectation.** This is the single most useful habit. The expectations are policy prose. The sample problems are actual mathematics, written in normal mathematical language, and they show you precisely what the expectation means in practice. Roughly two thirds of the expectations have one. When an expectation baffles you, skip to its sample problem and it will usually become obvious in seconds.

## Decoder for the recurring phrases

| Curriculum phrase | What it means |
|---|---|
| through investigation | The student should discover it rather than be told it. A teaching instruction, not content. Ignore it when you are auditing what you know. |
| using technology | Graphing calculator, Desmos, GeoGebra, spreadsheet. Often optional in practice. |
| make connections between X and Y | Understand that X and Y are the same thing seen from two angles. |
| in a variety of ways | Numerically, graphically, algebraically and in words. The four representations. |
| represented in a variety of ways | Same thing: table, graph, equation, sentence. |
| the roles of the parameters a, k, d, c | Transformations. Stretches, compressions, reflections, translations. |
| key features | Domain, range, intercepts, asymptotes, maxima and minima, end behaviour. |
| demonstrate an understanding of | Know it. |
| pose problems and solve them | Word problems. |
| recognize | Know it, but you will not be asked to prove it. |
| verify | Check it works in examples. Not a proof. |
| verify graphically and algebraically | Confirm two ways. Still not a proof. |

## The second reason it looks unfamiliar

Content genuinely has moved since you were at school. See `oac-to-now.md`. A significant amount of what you learned in the OAC courses is no longer taught anywhere in this stream, and some of what is taught now was not emphasized then. So part of your non-recognition is real, not linguistic. The file separates the two.
