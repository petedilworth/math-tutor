# Tutoring Sebastian

## What I do not know yet

Three things change this plan materially and I have asked about them but do not have answers:

1. Has he taken MHF4U, and when? It is the real prerequisite for MCV4U, not MCR3U. If he is taking it concurrently, the plan is different again, and that is common.
2. Is MCV4U in progress now, or starting later? That decides between pre-teaching and catch-up.
3. What are his marks, and what does he say went wrong? A 60 earned by an anxious student who understands the concepts is a different problem from a 60 earned by a student with algebra gaps.

The plan below works under either timing. Where it forks, I say so.

## First session: diagnose, do not teach

Resist the urge to start with content. Spend the first session finding out what is actually broken. You will otherwise spend six weeks teaching the wrong thing.

Run the diagnostic in `diagnostic.md`. It takes about forty minutes and targets the skills the prerequisite map identifies as load-bearing.

While he works, watch for the things a mark sheet cannot show you. Does he write the next line, or stare? Does he check his answer? When stuck, does he try something or stop? Does he reach for the calculator before thinking? Those behaviours predict his performance in MCV4U better than his MCR3U mark does.

Ask him directly what he finds hardest and what he thinks went wrong before. Students are often right about this and are rarely asked.

## Then follow his course, not your plan

If MCV4U is running, teach what his class is doing this week, plus the prerequisite repair the diagnostic identified, folded in underneath. Do not run a parallel curriculum. He has homework, tests and a teacher, and a tutor who competes with that is a burden.

The exception is the two weeks before a unit on vectors, where pre-teaching genuinely helps because nothing in his background supports it.

If MCV4U has not started, use the time on stage 1 and 2 material from the re-learning plan: the algebra, then rate of change. Those transfer directly and he will arrive ahead.

## The three places students lose marks

From the structure of the course, these are where to concentrate.

**The difference quotient, MCV4U A1.5 and A1.6.** Nearly always an algebra failure, not a calculus one. If he cannot simplify it, go back to MCR3U A3.3 and fix rational expressions. Do not explain limits harder.

**Curve sketching, MCV4U B1.5.** Requires holding several things at once: f′ sign, f″ sign, intercepts, end behaviour. Students who can do each piece still fail the synthesis. Teach it as a fixed checklist he executes in the same order every time, and make him do it the same way twenty times. This is one of the few places where drilling a procedure beats teaching for understanding.

**Planes and their intersections, MCV4U C4.4 and C4.7.** The algebra is elimination, which he can do. The difficulty is spatial: seeing what a solution means geometrically. Use physical objects. Three sheets of cardboard will teach the configurations of three planes faster than any diagram on paper, and the curriculum itself suggests exactly this in C3.3.

## Vectors is where to build his confidence

Strand C has almost no prerequisite in Grade 11 or MHF4U. Whatever went wrong before, it did not damage his ability to do vectors.

If he arrives demoralized, and students in this position often do, starting with vectors gives him a real success that is not a participation prize. It is close to half the course and it is genuinely open to him. Calculus, by contrast, will keep reminding him of every gap he has.

If his class is doing calculus first, you cannot reorder his course, but you can tell him this. Knowing that half the course starts fresh changes how a struggling student approaches the other half.

## Session shape

Sixty to ninety minutes. Longer does not work for most students.

- Ten minutes: he shows you what he did since last time and what went wrong. He talks, you listen.
- Twenty-five minutes: the day's content, worked by him with you prompting. You should be writing less than he is.
- Twenty minutes: problems he does alone while you watch, saying nothing unless he is stuck for more than about a minute.
- Five minutes: he tells you what the session was about. If he cannot, the session did not land, and you know before the next test rather than after it.

The silent watching is the part tutors find hardest and it is where most of the value is. You cannot diagnose a student whose work you have never seen unaided.

## What to measure

Not whether he understood in the session. Everyone understands in the session, with you sitting there.

Measure whether he can do last week's problem type cold this week, without prompting. Keep three or four problems from earlier sessions and spring them without warning. That is the only honest signal you will get between tests.
